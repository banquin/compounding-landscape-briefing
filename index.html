The Compounding Pharmacy Landscape

01

The value chain, end to end

Compounding is the practice of making a customized medication for an individual patient when an off-the-shelf drug won't do. A handful of distinct businesses sit between the raw chemical and the patient. Suppliers like PCCA sit upstream — they never touch a patient. They arm the pharmacies that do.

PCCA's footprint spans two layers most rivals don't: it supplies the ingredients and owns a piece of the software and testing that surround the pharmacy.

02

Where PCCA sits — and its business lines

PCCA (Professional Compounding Centers of America) is a membership organization for compounding pharmacies: members get ingredients, proprietary formulations, equipment, accredited education, and support. Founded in 1981, headquartered in Houston, it operates across the U.S., Canada, Australia, and the U.K., and is one of the three leading ingredient suppliers to independent compounders. It is privately held; its internal revenue split is not publicly disclosed, so the size labels below are directional, not exact.

Pharmacy practice-management software (The Compounder Rx), acquired in 2010 and historically the most widely used dedicated compounding system. A comparatively small line relative to chemicals — and the one being modernized to SaaS.

The strategic logic: own the proprietary chemicals, the formulas, the testing, and the software the pharmacy runs on — so the software becomes a distribution and lock-in layer for high-margin ingredients, not a standalone product.

03

How big, and growing or shrinking?

Growing — steadily, not explosively. The U.S. compounding market is roughly $6–7 billion and expanding at about 6–7% a year by most third-party estimates (figures vary by source and definition). The catch: the last two years were inflated by a one-time GLP-1 tailwind that has now largely closed (see §06).

Pharmacy software — PK is a niche specialist

The broad independent-pharmacy software market is led by RedSail Technologies (PioneerRx, QS/1, BestRx), which serves 10M+ patients monthly. PK is historically the most-used dedicated compounding system, but it's a small fish in the larger pond and is legacy/on-prem while cloud-native challengers (e.g., LifeFile) rise. 05

The rules: 503A vs 503B

The single most important structural fact in this industry. After a 2012 contamination tragedy (the New England Compounding Center meningitis outbreak), the Drug Quality and Security Act of 2013 split compounders into two regulated categories. This determines who PCCA's customers are: overwhelmingly 503A independents.

503A — traditional

  • MakesPatient-specific prescriptions, one at a time, on receipt of an Rx.
  • WhoMostly independent community pharmacies — the large, fragmented base.
  • RegulatorState boards of pharmacy; follows USP <795>/<797>/<800>.
  • ScaleNo large batches; cannot mass-produce or sell for "office use."
  • StandardNot required to follow full cGMP.
  • Revenue~$5–7B/yr (est.) — the bulk of industry dollars, spread thinly across ~7,500 pharmacies.
  • Growth~5–7% CAGR in the underlying base; the 2022–24 GLP-1 surge added a temporary $1–2B that's now unwinding.

503B — outsourcing

  • MakesBulk batches, with or without a patient-specific prescription.
  • WhoFDA-registered outsourcing facilities (~80–100 nationally); more industrial.
  • RegulatorFDA, federally; must register and undergo FDA inspection.
  • ScaleSells to hospitals/clinics for office use; the fastest-growing slice by revenue.
  • StandardFull cGMP — the same manufacturing standard as big pharma.
  • Revenue~$2–4B/yr (est.) across ~80–100 facilities — smaller in total dollars, but far higher revenue per site.
  • Growth~11–14% CAGR — the fastest-growing slice, pulled by hospital office-use demand and outsourcing of sterile production.

503B outsourcing — base revenue

The growth engine. Hospitals keep outsourcing sterile, office-use production to fewer, larger cGMP facilities, and consolidation concentrates volume. Revenue per facility dwarfs the typical 503A, so a small number of sites drive an outsized share of new dollars — but it's a capital-intensive, inspection-heavy game with high barriers to entry.

The strategic read for PK: industry dollars are growing fastest in the 503B/outsourcing tier and in telehealth-owned capacity — not in the independent 503A segment PK primarily serves.
06

Demand dynamics: the GLP-1 wave and what's next

Understanding recent growth means understanding the GLP-1 story — a boom that became a regulatory cliff — and the next waves (peptides, hormones) plus the structural threat of telehealth disintermediation.

2022
Shortage opens the door
Semaglutide and tirzepatide land on the FDA shortage list. Shortage status lets compounders legally make low-cost copies — a massive new revenue stream for pharmacies and the telehealth platforms feeding them.
Oct 2024
Tirzepatide shortage declared resolved
The FDA removes tirzepatide from the shortage list, starting the wind-down of legal compounding. Litigation follows but courts decline to block it.
Feb 2025
Semaglutide shortage declared resolved
Same path for semaglutide. Phased enforcement deadlines run through April–May 2025, after which large-scale compounding of "essentially a copy" is no longer permissible for 503A or 503B.
2025
The gray-area workaround
Roughly 80% of compounded GLP-1 scripts begin adding supplemental ingredients (e.g., B-vitamins) to argue they aren't exact copies — legally precarious, and not a durable base.
2026
The next waves: peptides & hormones
Regulators signal a looser stance on peptides (moving many off Category 2), and hormone therapy grows after a 2025 estrogen boxed-warning removal. Real new categories — but increasingly captured by vertically integrated telehealth players (Hims & Hers, Ro, LifeMD) building their own compounding capacity.

The tide is rising for compounding. It's less clear it's rising for independent compounding — the exact segment PK serves — as telehealth platforms consolidate demand and own more of the pharmacy.
