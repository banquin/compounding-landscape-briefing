01

The value chain, end to end

Compounding is the practice of making a customized medication for an individual patient when an off-the-shelf drug won't do. A handful of distinct businesses sit between the raw chemical and the patient. Suppliers like PCCA sit upstream — they never touch a patient. They arm the pharmacies that do.

Makers of bulk active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs) and raw chemicals — often overseas, sold into the supply layer.

Cross-cutting · Software — practice-management systems that run the pharmacy (PK; RedSail's PioneerRx and, since 2026, PrimeRx…), plus a new AI-automation layer on top (TJM Labs became PCCA's automation partner in June 2026). This is where PK lives.
Cross-cutting · Quality — testing labs & accreditation (PCCA's Eagle Analytical lab; ACHC/PCAB accreditation, plus an APC–CCE option since January 2026) that verify potency, sterility, and stability.

PCCA's footprint now spans three layers most rivals don't: it supplies the ingredients, owns a piece of the software and testing that surround the pharmacy, and — through sister company Wilcrest Pharma — has a foot in 503B production.

02

Where PCCA sits — and its business lines

PCCA (Professional Compounding Centers of America) is a membership organization for compounding pharmacies: members get ingredients, proprietary formulations, equipment, accredited education, and support. Founded in 1981, headquartered in Houston, it operates across the U.S., Canada, Australia, and the U.K., and is one of the three leading ingredient suppliers to independent compounders. Since 2025 it has been reorganized: Gus Bassani, PharmD — with PCCA since 2002, previously chief scientific officer — became CEO of the U.S. business on April 1, 2025, and PCCA now sits under a parent holding company, Precision Health Holdings (CEO Jim Smith), alongside Eagle Analytical, the Wilcrest Pharma 503B facility, and consumer incubator Curive Healthcare. Long-time president and CEO L. David Sparks, who had become the parent's chairman, died on May 9, 2026. PCCA is privately held; its internal revenue split is not publicly disclosed, so the size labels below are directional, not exact.

Core engine

Chemicals & APIs

The heart of the business: sourcing, testing, and selling bulk active ingredients and excipients to member pharmacies. This — together with education — is where the company makes most of its money. 2026 watch items: the FDA has vowed to restrict GLP-1 ingredients destined for unapproved compounded drugs, and peptide APIs are the next product wave.

Core engine

Education & clinical support

The PCCA Institute: training, webinars, symposiums, 24/7 clinical/formulation help, and a proprietary formula database. A moat that keeps members dependent and loyal. Newest program: Compounding PATH for Technicians, launched with NCPA and PTCB in November 2025 ($2,495 per technician; two 2026 cohorts).

Major line

Proprietary bases & formulations

Higher-margin, exclusive delivery technologies — e.g., SubMagna™ SL HMW, a sublingual base for high-molecular-weight drugs like semaglutide, and ExoBlue™ (May 2026), a cosmetic topical base aimed at personalized skincare. Moves the company up the value chain from commodity chemicals to differentiated IP — and, with ExoBlue, sideways into non-GLP-1 categories.

Major line

Equipment & supplies

The hardware of compounding: balances, mixers, capsule machines, devices, and consumables sold alongside the chemicals.

Adjacent

Eagle Analytical (testing lab)

An FDA-registered, ISO 17025-accredited analytical lab providing potency, sterility, and stability testing plus beyond-use dating — selling quality assurance into the same customer base. In June 2026 it partnered with accreditor ACHC, and it has flagged expansion into Canada, Florida, Arizona, and Australia.

Smaller / strategic

PK Software

Pharmacy practice-management software (The Compounder Rx), acquired in 2010 and historically the most widely used dedicated compounding system. A comparatively small line relative to chemicals. As of September 2026 no cloud relaunch has been announced publicly; PCCA's visible software move was instead an AI-automation partnership with TJM Labs (June 2026), layered on top of whatever system members already run.

Sister company · 503B

Wilcrest Pharma

A Houston-based, FDA-registered 503B outsourcing facility in the Precision Health Holdings family, focused on hazardous and oncology sterile and non-sterile medications for health systems (CEO A.J. Day, PharmD). Won a Premier, Inc. group-purchasing agreement effective April 1, 2026. This is PCCA's family stepping from supplying pharmacies into producing for hospitals.

Sister company · DTC

Curive Healthcare

A consumer-products incubator that uses PCCA's formulation expertise to build direct-to-consumer brands — first out is Plum, an intimate-skincare line. Small today, but it signals ambition to reach consumers without a pharmacy in between.

The strategic logic: own the proprietary chemicals, the formulas, the testing, and the software the pharmacy runs on — so the software becomes a distribution and lock-in layer for high-margin ingredients, not a standalone product. The 2025 holding-company structure adds two hedges against a squeezed independent channel: a 503B facility that sells to hospitals, and a consumer-brand incubator.

03

How big, and growing or shrinking?

Growing — steadily, not explosively. Third-party estimates put the U.S. compounding market at roughly $6–7.5 billion in 2026, expanding at about 6–7% a year toward $11.5–12.8 billion by 2035 (figures vary by source and definition; one broad-scope estimate runs above $20 billion). The catch: 2023–25 were inflated by a one-time GLP-1 tailwind that has now closed — and by mid-2026 the unwind was visible in supplier numbers, with Fagron citing "normalization of GLP-1 related revenues" in its North American compounding business (see §06).

Who the players are — and their relative size

Two different competitive maps matter: the ingredient-supplier layer where PCCA competes, and the pharmacy-software layer where PK competes. PCCA is a leader in the first; PK is a niche specialist in the second.

Ingredient suppliers — Fagron is the giant

Fagron is publicly traded and global, dwarfing the others on total revenue: €952M in FY2025 (+9.2%, twelve acquisitions in the year) and €552M in H1 2026 (+16% reported, but only +3.1% organic as U.S. GLP-1 compounding revenue normalized). It keeps buying in the U.S. — a California health-and-wellness 503A (UCP, September 2025) — and runs three 503B sterile sites with a Premier GPO contract since July 2025. PCCA and Medisca are the other two majors but are privately held, so exact revenue isn't public — they're shown indicatively. Letco (Fagron-owned) is included as a scale reference.

Pharmacy software — PK is a niche specialist

PK is historically the most-used dedicated compounding system, but it's a small fish in the larger pond and is legacy/on-prem while cloud-native challengers (e.g., LifeFile) rise — and a new AI-automation layer (TJM Labs, PCCA's official partner since June 2026; 400+ pharmacies, 300k scripts a day) is being bolted on top of systems rather than replacing them. 05

The rules: 503A vs 503B

The single most important structural fact in this industry. After a 2012 contamination tragedy (the New England Compounding Center meningitis outbreak), the Drug Quality and Security Act of 2013 split compounders into two regulated categories. This determines who PCCA's customers are: overwhelmingly 503A independents.

503A — traditional

  • MakesPatient-specific prescriptions, one at a time, on receipt of an Rx.
  • WhoMostly independent community pharmacies — the large, fragmented base.
  • RegulatorState boards of pharmacy; follows USP <795>/<797>/<800>.
  • ScaleNo large batches; cannot mass-produce or sell for "office use."
  • StandardNot required to follow full cGMP.
  • Revenue~$5–7B/yr (est.) — the bulk of industry dollars, spread thinly across ~7,500 pharmacies.
  • Growth~5–7% CAGR in the underlying base; the 2022–25 GLP-1 surge added a temporary $1–2B that is now unwinding — only narrow, documented, patient-specific GLP-1 compounding remains defensible.

503B — outsourcing

  • MakesBulk batches, with or without a patient-specific prescription.
  • WhoFDA-registered outsourcing facilities (93 on the FDA list in mid-2025 — call it 90–100); more industrial.
  • RegulatorFDA, federally; must register and undergo FDA inspection.
  • ScaleSells to hospitals/clinics for office use; the fastest-growing slice by revenue.
  • StandardFull cGMP — the same manufacturing standard as big pharma.
  • Revenue~$2–4B/yr (est.) across ~90–100 facilities — smaller in total dollars, but far higher revenue per site.
  • Growth~11–14% CAGR on the underlying hospital/office-use base — but 2026 is a reset year: the FDA's April 2026 proposal would shut off 503B GLP-1 volume, and several facilities (e.g., ProRx, BPI Labs) have already exited GLP-1 production.
Regulatory watch · what moved in 2025–26
  • Apr 30, 2026 The FDA proposed excluding semaglutide, tirzepatide, and liraglutide from the 503B bulks list — "no clinical need" for outsourcing facilities to compound them. Comments closed July 30; a final decision was pending at this update.
  • Aug 27, 2026 The Fifth Circuit rejected the Outsourcing Facilities Association's challenge to the FDA's shortage-resolution decisions — the trade group's last legal route to reopen mass GLP-1 compounding.
  • Feb 6, 2026 The FDA said it would use "all available compliance and enforcement tools," including restricting GLP-1 ingredients headed for unapproved compounded drugs — a supplier-layer lever, not just a pharmacy one. An import alert already limits GLP-1 APIs to a vetted "green list" of foreign makers.
  • Mar–Jun 2026 Warning-letter waves to telehealth marketers (30 on March 3; 25 more in June) plus an April 1 warning to 503B facilities. Legitimate 503A pharmacies filling patient-specific prescriptions were not the target.
  • Apr 22 · Jul 23–24, 2026 Peptides: 12 removed from Category 2, then the advisory committee backed six for the 503A bulks list over FDA staff objections. Not yet legal to compound — rulemaking (typically 6–12 months) must follow.
  • Nov 10, 2025 The FDA began removing boxed warnings from menopausal hormone therapy — a tailwind for the largest compounding category, even though regulators still steer patients toward approved products.
  • Jan 26, 2026 APC and the Coalition for Compounding Excellence launched a USP-based accreditation option for 503A pharmacies, an alternative to PCAB/ACHC.

The growth engine. Hospitals keep outsourcing sterile, office-use production to fewer, larger cGMP facilities, and consolidation concentrates volume. 06

Demand dynamics: the GLP-1 wave and what's next

Understanding recent growth means understanding the GLP-1 story — a boom that became a regulatory cliff and, in 2026, a closed door — plus the next waves (peptides, hormones) and the structural threat of telehealth disintermediation. Timeline updated through September 2026.

2022
Shortage opens the door
Semaglutide and tirzepatide land on the FDA shortage list. Shortage status lets compounders legally make low-cost copies — a massive new revenue stream for pharmacies and the telehealth platforms feeding them.
Oct–Dec 2024
Tirzepatide shortage declared resolved
The FDA removes tirzepatide from the shortage list in October and, after a trade-group lawsuit, reaffirms the decision in December. Courts decline to block it.
Feb 2025
Semaglutide shortage declared resolved
Same path for semaglutide. Phased enforcement deadlines run through April–May 2025, after which large-scale compounding of "essentially a copy" is no longer permissible for 503A or 503B. Lilly (April) and Novo sue telehealth sellers; Novo's first, short-lived Hims partnership collapses in June.
2025
The gray-area workaround — and the ad crackdown
Roughly 80% of compounded GLP-1 scripts add supplemental ingredients (e.g., B-vitamins) or "personalized" doses to argue they aren't exact copies. From September the FDA starts sending what it later calls "thousands" of letters over misleading compounded-GLP-1 advertising.
Nov–Dec 2025
Brands undercut the compounders
White House deals (Nov 6) price Wegovy and Zepbound at ~$350/month cash and the Wegovy pill at $149 through TrumpRx; the FDA approves that pill on Dec 22 — the first oral GLP-1 for weight loss. Separately (Nov 10), the FDA moves to strip boxed warnings from menopausal hormone therapy.
Jan–Mar 2026
The Hims episode and the telehealth pivot
Novo's CEO estimates up to 1.5 million Americans are on compounded GLP-1s (~$199/month). Hims launches a $49 compounded semaglutide pill on Feb 5; the FDA vows to restrict GLP-1 ingredients for unapproved compounding, HHS refers Hims to the DOJ, and the pill is pulled within two days. On March 9 Novo and Hims settle: Hims sells branded Wegovy/Ozempic and stops advertising compounded GLP-1s. Ro exits compounded GLP-1s; LifeMD never sold them. Thirty FDA warning letters go to telehealth firms on March 3.
Apr 2026
Doors close, doors open
Lilly's Foundayo (orforglipron) pill is approved (Apr 1); a judge lets Lilly's suit against telehealth seller Mochi proceed; the FDA warns 503B facilities (Apr 1), removes 12 peptides from Category 2 (Apr 22), and proposes excluding semaglutide, tirzepatide, and liraglutide from the 503B bulks list (Apr 30).
Jul–Aug 2026
Peptides advance; GLP-1 litigation ends
The FDA's advisory committee votes for six of seven peptides (BPC-157 by 8–6) to join the 503A bulks list — non-binding, rulemaking pending. Fagron says it has 20 peptide products lined up, five launching in H2 2026. Hims' Q2 shows the cost of the pivot (gross margin 64% vs 76%) and a push into peptides. On Aug 27 the Fifth Circuit upholds the FDA's shortage decisions.
Next
What to watch
The FDA's final 503B bulks decision; peptide rulemaking (and which suppliers are ready with validated APIs and bases); whether the 1.5 million compounded-GLP-1 patients migrate to $149–$350 branded options or to "personalized" 503A scripts; and HRT growth after the boxed-warning removal — the one next wave already sized (36% of compounded therapies).

The tide is still rising for compounding — but the GLP-1 flood has drained back to the brands, and the telehealth platforms that once fed independents now sell Wegovy and Zepbound directly. The next waves (peptides, hormones) are real, smaller, and contested. For the independent compounding PK serves, 2026 is a reset, not a windfall.
